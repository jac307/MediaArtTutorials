# Student-Led Critical Discussion

## Team activity · Teams of three

## Goal

Lead a focused class discussion that connects the assigned weekly reading with the assigned artist or artwork.

Each class session will include two teams:

- **Context Crew:** Introduces the reading, explains key concepts, and analyzes the assigned artist or artwork.
- **Conversation Crew:** Facilitates critical discussion and leads a structured, non-digital activity.

> Attendance, preparation, participation, and active engagement during the discussion are part of the activity grade.

## Primary-material requirement

Both crews must use the **assigned weekly reading and assigned artist or artwork as the primary materials** for presentation, analysis, questions, and activities.

Additional artists, artworks, films, readings, or examples may be included when they help clarify a concept. However, these examples must remain **secondary** and must not replace sustained engagement with the assigned materials.

## Activity overview

- **Group size:** Three students
- **Format:** In-person presentation and discussion
- **Teams per class:** Two
- **Context Crew duration:** 20–25 minutes
- **Conversation Crew duration:** 20–25 minutes
- **Equipment:** Use your own computer; HDMI adapters will be available
- **Context Crew submission:** Slides and supporting materials as PDF
- **Conversation Crew submission:** Completed Discussion Plan and supporting materials as PDF
- **Submission deadline:** One day before the presentation
- **Enrolment:** Select one available week and one crew role in advance

## Learning objectives

By completing this activity, students will:

- Collaborate effectively as a team
- Summarize and interpret theoretical and artistic materials
- Connect critical concepts with specific formal and creative decisions
- Analyze how theory operates within film and media artworks
- Facilitate inclusive and focused class discussion
- Translate critical analysis into questions, activities, and creative-practice takeaways

<details class="tutorial-section" id="context-crew">
  <summary>
    <span class="section-title">2. Context Crew</span>
    <span class="section-description">
      Present the assigned reading’s central concepts and analyze how they operate in the assigned artist or artwork.
    </span>
  </summary>

<div class="section-content" markdown="1">

## Duration

```text
20–25 minutes
```

## Primary focus

The presentation must focus primarily on:

- The assigned weekly reading
- The assigned artist or artwork
- Specific connections between the two

Additional examples are welcome, but they must remain secondary. They should clarify, compare, or extend the analysis rather than replacing the assigned example.

## Introduce the author

Use a maximum of **one slide** to introduce:

- The author
- Relevant background
- The context of the reading
- Why the text matters for film, media, or artistic practice

Keep this section concise. The presentation should focus on the reading’s ideas rather than a detailed biography.

## Explain the key concepts

Identify and explain **three or four central arguments or concepts** from the assigned reading.

For each concept:

- Define it clearly.
- Explain it in your own words.
- Refer to a specific passage or example from the reading.
- Explain why it matters for media analysis or creative practice.

Do not attempt to summarize every section of the text.

## Analyze the assigned artist or artwork

Make **one or two developed connections** between the reading and the assigned artist or artwork.

Your analysis may examine:

- How the artwork embodies a concept from the reading
- How the artwork supports, complicates, or resists the author’s argument
- How sound, editing, framing, rhythm, duration, performance, or camera movement relates to the reading
- How the artist’s methods align with or challenge the author’s perspective
- How cultural, social, political, or historical context affects the relationship between theory and practice

Use specific moments, scenes, formal choices, or artistic decisions as evidence.

> The assigned artist or artwork must provide the primary examples. Added examples may support the explanation, but they must not become the main subject of the presentation.

## Present the key takeaways

Conclude with two or three concise takeaways.

Address:

- What should the class remember from the reading?
- What does the assigned artwork help us understand?
- How might these ideas inform this week’s creative practice?

## Delivery requirements

- Present in person.
- Use clear slides or other appropriate media.
- Bring the presentation on your own computer.
- Organize speaking responsibilities across all three team members.
- Remain within 20–25 minutes.
- Submit the slides and supporting materials as PDF files one day before the presentation.

<details class="tutorial-section" id="conversation-crew">
  <summary>
    <span class="section-title">3. Conversation Crew</span>
    <span class="section-description">
      Facilitate critical dialogue and lead a structured, non-digital activity based on the assigned reading and artwork.
    </span>
  </summary>

<div class="section-content" markdown="1">

## Duration

```text
20–25 minutes
```

## Primary focus

The discussion questions and activity must focus primarily on:

- The assigned weekly reading
- The assigned artist or artwork
- The relationship between the theoretical ideas and artistic practice

Additional examples may be introduced when they help the class compare or clarify an idea. These examples must remain secondary and should not redirect the discussion away from the assigned materials.

## Prepare the discussion questions

Create **three to five questions** that invite analysis, interpretation, reflection, or debate.

Questions should:

- Connect directly to the assigned reading
- Refer to the assigned artist or artwork
- Move beyond factual recall or summary
- Allow multiple supported interpretations
- Encourage students to use specific concepts or artistic evidence
- Connect theory with formal and creative decisions

Avoid questions that can be answered with only “yes,” “no,” or a definition.

## Design a non-digital activity

Create at least **one structured activity** that does not require computers, cellphones, or tablets.

Possible formats include:

- Small-group close analysis
- Printed image or sequence analysis
- Concept-matching cards
- Timeline or shot-order reconstruction
- Position-taking exercise
- Collaborative annotation
- Short written reflection
- Role-based debate
- Physical mapping or sorting activity
- Creative-response prompt using paper materials

The activity must:

- Have a clear purpose
- Relate directly to the assigned reading and artwork
- Include clear instructions
- Fit within the available time
- Lead into or support the group discussion

## Plan inclusive participation

Use strategies such as:

- Small groups before whole-class discussion
- Assigned speaking roles
- Turn-taking
- Written reflection before speaking
- Prompts for quieter participants
- Multiple ways to contribute
- Clear time limits
- Respectful redirection when discussion moves off topic

## Facilitate the discussion

During the activity:

- Introduce the purpose and instructions clearly.
- Keep track of time.
- Ask follow-up questions.
- Invite evidence from the assigned reading and artwork.
- Redirect the conversation to the core concepts when needed.
- Connect student responses to one another.
- Summarize important points before moving forward.

Use the [Discussion Plan Template](Discussion-Plan-Template.docx){:target="_blank"} to organize the session.

## Delivery requirements

- Facilitate the discussion in person.
- Bring any slides, media, or printable activity materials on your own computer.
- Bring printed materials when the activity requires them.
- Organize facilitation responsibilities across all three team members.
- Remain within 20–25 minutes.
- Submit the completed Discussion Plan and supporting materials as PDFs one day before the presentation.

## Submission deadline

Each team must submit its materials **one day before the scheduled presentation**.

Submitted materials may be shared with the class and will be used for assessment.

## Context Crew submission

Submit:

- Presentation slides as a PDF
- Any supporting materials or handouts as PDFs
- Any required links to audiovisual examples

The presentation must use the assigned reading and assigned artist or artwork as the primary materials.

## Conversation Crew submission

Submit:

- Completed Discussion Plan Template as a PDF
- Discussion questions
- Activity instructions
- Printable activity materials or handouts as PDFs
- Any required links to audiovisual examples

The discussion plan and activity must use the assigned reading and assigned artist or artwork as the primary materials.

## Assessment criteria: Context Crew

| Criterion | Weight | Expectations |
|---|---:|---|
| **Depth of understanding** | 2.5% | Accurately explains the reading’s main arguments and concepts |
| **Theory–practice connections** | 2.5% | Develops meaningful connections with the assigned artist or artwork |
| **Clarity and accessibility** | 2.5% | Uses clear language, logical organization, readable slides, and relevant evidence |
| **Audience engagement** | 2.5% | Uses effective delivery, pacing, examples, and engagement strategies |

## Assessment criteria: Conversation Crew

| Criterion | Weight | Expectations |
|---|---:|---|
| **Activity design and relevance** | 2.5% | Creates a purposeful activity grounded in the assigned reading and artwork |
| **Facilitation of critical thinking** | 2.5% | Uses questions and follow-ups that move beyond summary |
| **Preparation and planning** | 2.5% | Demonstrates clear structure, time management, materials, roles, and backup planning |
| **Discussion leadership** | 2.5% | Facilitates inclusive participation and responds constructively to the discussion |

> In both roles, using unrelated examples as the main focus instead of the assigned reading and assigned artist or artwork will weaken the depth, relevance, and theory–practice connection of the work.
