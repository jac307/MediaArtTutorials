## P2: Research-Creation Project · Groups of 3–4

# Technology Experiment

**Goal:** Learn and test the unfamiliar technology or technique selected in Version 1. Use focused experiments to determine what it can contribute to the project, what limits or risks it introduces, and how it should be used in the first artistic prototype.

> This submission evaluates research, experimentation, documentation, and reflection. A technically polished or finished artwork is not expected.

## What you will complete

- Individual technology-learning logs
- A documented set of three or four focused experiments
- Evidence of successful, unsuccessful, and unexpected results
- A group evaluation of the primary technology or technique
- A decision to continue, modify, combine, or replace it
- A revised plan for the first artistic prototype
- A ZIP package containing the report, individual logs, documentation, and available experiment files
- A one-slide rapid presentation

## Division of labour

Every member must personally use the unfamiliar technology or technique and document one hands-on test.

| Member | Learning task | Hands-on test | Documentation task | Prototype-planning task |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Member 1 | Assigned tutorial or process | Test 1 | Assigned evidence | Assigned contribution |
| Member 2 | Assigned tutorial or process | Test 2 | Assigned evidence | Assigned contribution |
| Member 3 | Assigned tutorial or process | Test 3 | Assigned evidence | Assigned contribution |
| Member 4, if applicable | Assigned tutorial or process | Test 4 | Assigned evidence | Assigned contribution |

All members must understand the complete workflow, results, limitations, and decision before submitting.

> Assigning one technically experienced member to complete all production does not meet the learning requirement. The selected process should remain unfamiliar to every member at the beginning of this stage.

## Review the Version 1 technology plan

Review:

- The primary technology or technique
- The backup option
- The official documentation and tutorial selected in Version 1
- The two or three technical questions identified by the group
- Required software, equipment, materials, accounts, or spaces
- Initial concerns involving cost, access, compatibility, safety, privacy, consent, or environmental impact

Revise the questions if they are too broad to answer through a small experiment.

Examples of focused questions:

- Can the tool produce the required image, sound, movement, interaction, or material effect?
- Which settings or materials produce a usable result?
- How accurately or reliably does the process respond?
- What delay, resolution, scale, duration, or compatibility limit affects the work?
- Can the intended audience encounter the result safely and accessibly?
- Does the process collect, transform, store, or expose personal data?

## Confirm access and feasibility

Before testing, confirm:

- The technology or technique is available to the group.
- Required software is installed and opens correctly.
- Equipment, rooms, or materials have been booked or gathered.
- File formats and devices are compatible.
- The first tests can be completed within the available time.
- Safety instructions have been reviewed.
- Consent and privacy requirements have been addressed before recording or collecting data from anyone.

If the primary option cannot be accessed, document the specific barrier and begin testing the backup option. Do not switch only because the first process is difficult to learn.

## Complete the initial technical learning

Every member must complete an assigned part of the technical learning. Use official documentation, manuals, demonstrations, or reliable tutorials.

For each learning resource, record:

- Title, creator or organization, and working link
- Section, chapter, or timestamp completed
- Main steps or concepts learned
- New terminology, settings, tools, or safety procedures
- What remains unclear
- How the learning relates to the planned experiment

> Watching a tutorial without applying its process does not count as a hands-on experiment.

## Prepare the individual technology-learning log

Each member must prepare an individual log containing:

- [ ] Full name and student number
- [ ] Assigned learning resource and working link
- [ ] Notes on the process completed
- [ ] Description of the hands-on task personally attempted
- [ ] At least one screenshot, photograph, recording still, output, or other form of evidence
- [ ] What worked
- [ ] What did not work or produced an unexpected result
- [ ] One technical question that remains
- [ ] One skill to practise before the first prototype

The log may use notes, tables, screenshots, images, diagrams, or short paragraphs.

Name the file:

`Lastname-Firstname_P2_Technology-Experiment-Log.pdf`

Share the log with the complete group.

## Conduct three or four focused experiments

Each member must personally complete at least one hands-on test. Groups of three will complete at least three tests; groups of four will complete at least four tests.

The experiments may build on one another, but each test must examine a specific question, setting, material, interaction, or condition.

Possible variables include:

- Input material or data
- Software setting or parameter
- Tool, sensor, device, or physical material
- Scale, duration, distance, lighting, volume, speed, or resolution
- File format, output method, or presentation system
- Audience action or interaction
- Accessibility or safety condition

When possible, change one main variable at a time so the result can be compared with the previous test.

## Record each experiment

Use the following structure:

| Area | What to record |
|---|---|
| Test question | What the group is trying to find out |
| Connection | Relationship to the concept, artist, or intended audience experience |
| Setup | Software, equipment, materials, settings, files, space, and roles |
| Procedure | Main steps completed |
| Variable | What changed from another test |
| Evidence | Screenshots, photographs, recordings, exported files, code, samples, or observations |
| Result | What happened without exaggerating success or failure |
| Interpretation | What the result means for the proposed artwork |
| Limitation | Technical, conceptual, ethical, accessibility, cost, time, or safety concern |
| Next step | What should be repeated, changed, or tested next |

## Document unsuccessful and unexpected results

Do not remove failed attempts from the report. A useful experiment may show that:

- The selected tool cannot produce the intended result.
- A process takes longer or costs more than expected.
- Equipment or files are incompatible.
- An unexpected output offers a stronger artistic direction.
- The technology distracts from the concept.
- The process creates an accessibility, privacy, consent, or safety problem.

Explain what the group learned and how the finding will affect the next iteration.

## Connect the tests to the research

For each experiment, identify:

- An artistic strategy, method, or question connected to the selected artist
- A concept from the academic or course research completed in Version 1
- A specific way the result supports, changes, or challenges the concept proposal

The technology should contribute to the artistic and critical direction. Do not evaluate it only by whether it functions correctly.

## Record the experiment files

Save the evidence produced during the tests, including relevant:

- Screenshots or photographs
- Short video or audio documentation
- Exported images, audio, video, models, code, or other outputs
- Native or editable project files when available and reasonably sized
- Physical samples documented through clear photographs
- Notes about settings, versions, equipment, or materials needed to reproduce the result

Use short filenames without spaces or special characters.

For files too large for Avenue to Learn, include a stable viewing link in the report. Confirm that the instructor can open the link without requesting access.

## Compare the experiment results

As a group, review all individual logs and test evidence.

Discuss:

1. Which test answered its question most clearly?
2. Which result contributes most strongly to the project concept?
3. Which limitations can be addressed through further learning or production changes?
4. Which limitations require the concept, format, audience experience, or technology to change?
5. What did the experiments reveal that research alone did not show?
6. Does the technology remain appropriate for the artist connection and EDI concern?

## Make the technology decision

Select one decision and explain it using evidence from the experiments:

| Decision | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Continue | Use the primary technology as planned |
| Modify | Keep the technology but change its role, workflow, scale, or output |
| Combine | Use selected parts of the primary and backup options together |
| Replace | Move to the backup option because a documented barrier makes the primary option unsuitable |

Do not describe the decision only as a preference. Refer to specific tests, outputs, limitations, and research connections.

## Write the technology findings and project revision

Write **200–300 words** explaining:

- What the group tested
- The most important successful, unsuccessful, or unexpected result
- What the group learned about the technology or technique
- How the findings affect the concept, artistic strategy, audience experience, or ethical approach
- The group's decision to continue, modify, combine, or replace the technology
- What must be developed in the first artistic prototype

This is not a new complete concept proposal. Focus on what changed because of the hands-on experiments.

## Plan the first artistic prototype

Define the smallest prototype that can combine:

- The central project concept
- At least one strategy connected to the selected artist
- The selected technology or technique
- A testable part of the intended audience experience

Record:

| Area | Prototype plan |
|---|---|
| Purpose | What the prototype needs to demonstrate or help the group learn |
| Content | Images, sounds, movements, text, materials, data, or actions required |
| Technology | Selected tool, technique, settings, and intended role |
| Audience experience | What a viewer, listener, participant, or user will encounter or do |
| Scale and duration | Size, length, location, or scope of the prototype |
| Access and ethics | Consent, privacy, representation, accessibility, safety, or cultural context |
| Tasks | Work assigned to each member |
| Evidence | What the group will document for Version 2 |

Keep the prototype achievable within one week. It does not need to contain every planned element of the final artwork.

`P2_Technology-Experiment-Report.pdf`

## Two-minute in-class presentation

During the following lecture, each group will give a rapid **two-minute presentation**. Presentations will take place one after another, without questions or class feedback.

Prepare **one slide in image format** containing:

- Selected technology or technique
- Three or four test questions in brief form
- Representative evidence from at least two tests
- Most important successful, unsuccessful, or unexpected finding
- Decision to continue, modify, combine, or replace the technology
- The next step for the first artistic prototype

## Slide requirements

- Submit one `.JPG` or `.PNG` image.
- Use a 16:9 format, preferably `1920 × 1080 px`.
- Keep the text brief and readable.
- Caption the test images or outputs.
- Credit any material created outside the group.
- Choose a speaker who did not present Version 1.
- Do not exceed two minutes.
- Name the file `P2_Technology-Experiment-Slide.jpg` or `P2_Technology-Experiment-Slide.png`.

> Slides must be submitted in advance so they can be presented in sequence without changing computers.
